## 1. What Koha is, in one paragraph

Koha is a full integrated library system: it assumes the library is the whole institution, not a corner of it. Everything hangs off two record families — a *bibliographic record* (the title, described in MARC) and *item records* (the physical copies, each with its own barcode and status) — and one engine, the **circulation rules matrix**, which decides for every combination of library, patron category and item type how lending behaves: how many items, for how long, at what fine, with what grace, cap and suspension. Around that core sit a public catalogue (the OPAC) where patrons serve themselves, an acquisitions and serials operation for buying and tracking material, shelf tools (labels, stocktaking), and a reporting layer that can query the whole database. A school library module inside a school system is, by comparison, one desk of this building — which is exactly why Koha is worth studying for depth, and exactly why parts of it must deliberately not be chased (§5).

## 2. Koha's capability census, area by area (our own words)

**Cataloguing.** A title is stored as a MARC record (MARC21 or UNIMARC). Which fields and subfields exist is governed by *frameworks* — editable templates per material kind (books, DVDs, serials…), so a record only carries the fields that kind needs. Names and subjects are controlled by *authority records*: the cataloguer picks the authorised form of an author or subject heading instead of typing free text, and the system keeps every record using that heading consistent. Physical copies are separate *item records* hung off the title: each has its own barcode, item type, collection, shelf location, and a status vocabulary (available, on loan, lost, damaged, withdrawn, not-for-loan, in transit). New titles rarely need typing from scratch — a Z39.50/SRU search pulls a ready-made record from another library's catalogue and imports it ("copy cataloguing"), and there is a fast-cataloguing path for items that arrive without records. Batch import of staged record files is built in.

**Circulation — the desk.** Issue, return and renewal are barcode-first acts at a desk. Returning an item can trigger three things automatically: it can *trap* a hold (the copy is spoken for by the next patron in a queue), it can start a *transfer* to the branch that needs it, and it can surface the returning patron's fines. Due dates are not typed in; they are computed from the rules matrix plus the library's calendar of closed days. Koha also speaks SIP2, the protocol self-checkout machines and RFID pads use, and it can print issue receipts and produce a formal "discharge" proving a patron has nothing outstanding.

**The circulation and fines rules matrix — the heart.** Rules are keyed by **library × patron category × item type**, with an eight-step fallback from most specific ("this branch, this patron category, this item type") to least ("all libraries, all categories, all types"), so a school sets one default row and overrides only the exceptions. Each row carries: how many items the patron may have out now and how many on hold; the loan period and its unit (days *or hours* — reference material can circulate by the hour); an optional hard due date that overrides the period; the **fine amount**, the **fine charging interval** (charge every day, every two hours…), the **fine grace period** (days overdue before fines start), the **overdue fines cap** (a per-loan ceiling, so a lost-in-the-cupboard book cannot generate an unbounded fine); a **suspension threshold** — when a patron's unpaid fines pass a set amount, borrowing is suspended automatically until they pay; renewal limits (how many renewals, for how long, and whether a hold on the item blocks renewal); and whether automatic renewal is on. Overdue handling escalates through notice triggers: at configured overdue intervals the system sends progressively firmer notices.

**Holds (reserves).** A patron can hold a *title* (next available copy) or a *specific copy*. Holds form a priority queue per title; staff can reorder it, suspend a hold until a date (the patron keeps their place), set an expiry, or cancel. Two working lists drive the shelf work: the *holds queue* (which copy Koha has matched to which hold, rebuilt continuously) and *holds to pull* (the same work grouped by title for a shelf walk). When a copy is checked in and trapped, it goes to the *awaiting pickup* shelf; if it is not collected within the configured pickup window it expires (optionally with a fee) and passes to the next hold. Holds placed for pickup at another branch generate transfers, and the check-in desk confirms arrivals.

**Offline circulation.** Koha plans for the day the network or server dies, with three utilities. The built-in one lives in the browser: while online, staff open the offline page and *synchronise* — a local copy of patron and circulation data is downloaded into browser storage (with an honest size limit, a few MB, which is why larger libraries are steered to the other tools). A Firefox add-on and a standalone Windows tool do the same job against a locally generated borrower database file. While offline, staff keep issuing and returning by barcode; every act is written to a transaction file (the `.koc` file). When service returns, the files from *every* desk are uploaded first, then processed together — processing applies the stored transactions one by one, in date order across all desks (so a book issued at desk A and returned at desk B doesn't end up "still issued" because B's file was processed first), and reports a per-transaction outcome: *success*, *borrower not found*, *item not found*, *item not issued*. Failures stay visible as a list to fix by hand, rather than silently dropping or double-posting.

**The OPAC — patron self-service.** The public catalogue is a real storefront: keyword and advanced search with facets (by item type, location, availability), cover images, patron-built lists, and moderated tags and reviews. Signed-in patrons run their own desk: see current loans and **renew online**; opt in or out of automatic renewal; **place, suspend and cancel holds**; view their fines; suggest purchases for the library to consider; and set **messaging preferences** — which notices (advance due-date reminders, hold-available, overdue) arrive by email, SMS or print. The deepest piece is **reading history with privacy controls**: patrons see everything they have ever borrowed, and can choose whether that history is kept forever, kept for a default period, or never kept — in which case their returned loans are anonymised, and even staff lose sight of them. Patrons can also self-register (subject to staff approval) and update their own contact details.

**Acquisitions.** Buying is a workflow, not a spreadsheet: *vendors* carry contacts, discount and delivery terms; money lives in *budgets* split into *funds*, and every order encumbers its fund so the librarian always sees committed vs. spent vs. remaining. Orders are gathered into baskets — from catalogue records, from patron suggestions, or from files — sent, then received against shipments and invoices; late orders are *claimed* from the vendor from inside the system, and EDI lets large vendors take orders and return invoices electronically. Currencies and exchange rates are handled for foreign orders.

**Serials.** Periodicals get a subscription record per title: vendor, frequency, and a *numbering prediction pattern* (volume/issue increments, seasonality) that the librarian can test before saving. The system then predicts each expected issue; receiving is a one-click check-in of the predicted issue, unpredicted arrivals are handled as extras, and expected-but-missing issues are *claimed* from the vendor. Routing lists can circulate a new issue through a list of readers before it shelves.

**Labels, cards, and stocktaking.** A label creator designs layouts and templates (page geometry, fields, barcode format) and prints batches of spine labels and barcode labels from lists of items; a sibling creator does the same for patron cards. The inventory tool is built for the annual stocktake: staff scan the shelves (or upload a file of barcodes), and Koha compares what was seen against what the catalogue says should be there — updating each seen item's last-seen date, flagging items in the wrong place, and offering to mark unseen-but-expected items as lost.

**Course reserves.** Teachers' reading lists become a circulation construct: a *course* (department, instructor, term) gathers a reserve list of items; while on reserve, those items temporarily take on a different item type, location and loan period (short loans, library-use-only), then revert when the course ends. The OPAC lets students browse reserves by course or instructor.

**Interlibrary loans.** When the library doesn't own something, an ILL request — created by the patron in the OPAC or by staff — tracks the borrowing from another library through its statuses, with pluggable backends for the networks and partners a library actually uses.

**Patron management.** Patrons belong to *categories* that carry the business rules of membership: age ranges, enrolment period and expiry behaviour, enrolment fees, whether the category can be suspended, and the default messaging preferences its members start with. A patron record holds expiry dates (membership lapses and can lock OPAC access), restrictions applied by staff or automatically by the fines engine, custom attributes the library defines, and a full account ledger of fines, payments, write-offs and credits. Batch tools modify or anonymise patrons in bulk — anonymisation being the enforcement arm of the OPAC privacy promise.

**Reporting.** Beyond canned statistics (circulation counts, catalogue counts, patron counts), Koha's reporting is an open door: a six-step *guided wizard* builds a report from chosen fields, limits and calculations without code, and staff who can write SQL save their own queries — with runtime parameters, so one saved report asks "which branch? which month?" each time it runs. Saved reports are shared across the library's staff and can be scheduled.

**Where this leaves the ERP's "SOURCE-COMPLETE" library claim:** the claim survives for the *desk core* — loans, returns, reservations and fine entries are genuinely there, which is more than IQRS had before FC-7. But against Koha, "complete" means the first floor of the building: the ERP library has **no rules engine behind its fine entries, no renewals, no offline mode, no patron self-service, no stocktaking, no labels** — six capabilities a working school librarian uses weekly. The honest label is the audit's own qualifier: complete *core*, with the depth still open.

**Where IQRS stands, plainly:** the brief's premise is confirmed. After C1 + FC-7 the IQRS library is a competent basic desk — books with categories, card-carrying members, issue/return, a real reservation queue, registers — over a small live collection (14 books, 12 issues at last census, demo-seeded since). It has **no fine model, no loan rules, no renewals, no student-facing catalogue, no offline mode, no labels, no stocktaking**. Everything Koha-like about it stops at the desk counter.

## 4. Headline flagged list — what to adopt, and what it would take

1. ⭐ **A school-sized circulation + fines rules matrix** (both systems). One rules table keyed by member type (student / staff) × book category: loan days, max items out, fine per day, grace days, per-loan fine cap, and a borrowing-block threshold. Issue/return consult it; overdue fines compute from it and post into the existing fee ledger (IQRS: the AFM engine — never a parallel money store; ERP: the existing fine-entry rails). *Effort: one table + hooks in the issue/return path + a settings screen — days-to-a-week per system, the highest-value item on this list.*
2. ⭐ **Offline circulation on the in-house sync pattern** (both systems). The Workspace app already runs an offline outbox with idempotent push-sync for attendance and fees; the library desk needs the same shape: capture issue/return acts offline against a synced member/book snapshot, upload when back, process in timestamp order, and show a per-transaction outcome list (posted / member not found / book not found / already returned). *Effort: a new sync payload type on existing rails + a pending-actions screen — a small lane, not a new architecture.*
3. ⭐ **Student-facing catalogue + self-service in the portal/app** (both systems). Search the catalogue (title/author/category, availability), see own loans and fines, renew once if rules allow, place/cancel a reservation, choose notice channels. IQRS already has the portal library page (read-only) and the reservation engine (FC-7) — this is exposing them to the member, not building them. *Effort: portal/app surfaces over existing governed models + the rules from item 1.*
4. **Renewals** (both systems). A renew act at the desk and in self-service, bounded by the rules matrix (max renewals, blocked when a hold queue exists). *Effort: small — one act + rule checks, rides on items 1 and 3.*
5. **Per-copy catalogue records** (IQRS mainly; ERP to evidence/complete categories). Split title from copies: each copy gets a barcode and a status; the desk scans copies, not titles. *Effort: a copies table + migration of quantity into copy rows + desk flow change — a medium lane, and the enabler for items 6 and 2's book-level accuracy.*
6. **Spine/barcode label printing + stocktaking** (both systems). A label layout over copy barcodes (the print system already exists school-wide), and a stocktake mode: scan a shelf, compare against expected, flag missing, mark last-seen. *Effort: one print template + one scan-compare screen — small once item 5 lands; the annual stocktake is a real school ritual this directly serves.*
7. **Overdue notice escalation bound to the messaging rails** (both systems). Notice triggers at set overdue intervals, sent through the channels the school already governs (IQRS: CM1/Sendqon rails; ERP: once its channels actually send), respecting the member's channel preference. *Effort: a scheduled job + templates + preference field — small.*
8. **Reading history with a privacy switch** (both systems). Members see their borrowing history; a keep/anonymise choice mirrors Koha's — and slots straight into IQRS's FC-14 privacy module and the ERP's privacy line. *Effort: a history view + an anonymisation job — small.*
9. **Membership expiry at rollover + notice preferences** (both systems). Library membership lapses with the school year unless renewed at rollover; category defaults set notice channels. *Effort: a field + a rollover hook — very small.*
10. **A canned library statistics pack** (both systems). Most-issued titles, never-issued stock, overdue ageing, issues by class/category — as fixed reports, not a query builder. *Effort: a handful of governed queries on existing registers — small.*
11. **Acquisitions-lite** (both systems). A book-buying register — supplier, order, received-into-catalogue — reusing the inventory/supplier rails each system already has, so a received order *creates the book and its copies*. *Effort: a thin register + a receive-into-catalogue act — small-to-medium; the full budget/fund machinery is §5.*
12. **Textbook-set lending, reinterpreted from course reserves** (both systems, minor). Class sets of textbooks issued per class per term with a set-level return check — the school-shaped cousin of Koha's reserve lists. *Effort: a set construct over copies + a class issue/return flow — medium; schedule only if the school asks.*

**Flagged counts:** IQRS — **12 flagged items** (3 headline ⭐ + 9 supporting), 5 not-relevant. Rektrs ERP — **12 flagged items** (same 3 ⭐ + 9), 5 not-relevant. The lists converge because both libraries stop at the same desk; the ERP's head start is the loan/reservation/fine-entry core, IQRS's is the governed portal + privacy module the self-service and privacy items can hang off.

## 5. School fit — what is sized for one school, and what to deliberately NOT chase

**Sized for a single school library (adopt):** the rules matrix *subset* (two member types × a handful of categories is a matrix a librarian can hold in her head — Koha's eight-level branch fallback collapses to one default row plus exceptions); offline circulation (a school library desk with one PC and Pakistani connectivity realities is exactly the failure mode it exists for); portal self-service (students already live in the portal/app); renewals, labels, stocktaking, overdue notices, reading-history privacy. These are weekly-use capabilities at school scale.

**Public-library scale — deliberately NOT to chase:**
- **MARC frameworks, authority records, Z39.50 copy cataloguing.** This is the honest big one. MARC exists so libraries can *share* records with each other and with national catalogues; a school library buys its books from shops, not through a bibliographic utility, its catalogue is a few thousand titles keyed in once, and no second institution will ever import its records. A flat title record with author/publisher/ISBN/category fields serves a school completely. Adopting MARC would tax every future catalogue screen with fields nobody fills. **Verdict: skip permanently; revisit only if a multi-school consortium with a shared union catalogue ever becomes real.**
- **Serials prediction patterns and claiming.** The machinery exists for libraries holding hundreds of journal subscriptions. A school receives a handful of magazines; a plain periodicals register (title, supplier, issues received as a log) covers it. Prediction patterns and vendor claiming are ceremony without a problem.
- **Full acquisitions (budgets → funds → encumbrance, EDI, multi-currency).** Fund accounting belongs to institutions whose library budget is audited as its own ledger. A school's book buying rides its existing purchase/inventory rails; acquisitions-lite (item 11) is the ceiling.
- **Interlibrary loans and branch-transfer/consortia machinery.** Both presume a *network* of libraries. One school, one campus: there is nobody to borrow from and nowhere to transfer to. (If Rektrs ERP ever sells a *group of schools under one trust* a shared-catalogue conversation, that is a new product question — not a reason to build ILL now.)
- **SIP2 self-check hardware and RFID.** Real kit, real money, sized for high-throughput public desks. A barcode scanner at the librarian's desk is the school's ceiling.
- **A SQL/guided report builder for the library.** Koha's open reporting answers questions nobody predicted; a school asks the same six questions every term. Canned statistics (item 10) answer them without handing staff a query language.

## 6. Cross-check against the earlier studies — no double counting

- **RosarioSIS study, flagged item 7** (entry/exit checkpoints + scan-driven actions) already counts the idea that *a card scan performs a configured act — including lending a library document* — and RosarioSIS's add-on shelf noted a basic library (catalogue, loans, barcodes, overdue notifications). Koha's circulation is the same barcode-first desk carried to full depth; this study does **not** re-flag scan-driven lending. What Koha adds beyond RosarioSIS's library, and what is flagged here on Koha's own merit: the rules matrix, offline circulation with conflict outcomes, OPAC self-service with privacy controls, stocktaking. The overdue-notice flag (item 7) is the *escalation-engine* version — triggers, intervals, per-patron channel prefs — deeper than RosarioSIS's notification add-on, and counted here as the engine, not the notice.
- **Gibbon study** noted Gibbon's library (any loanable item, loans, students browsing and sharing their reading record) and its Resources bank. The reading-record idea therefore predates this study in the batch; what is flagged here (item 8) is only Koha's delta on it — the **privacy control and anonymisation** around the history, which neither Gibbon nor RosarioSIS has.
- **Moodle study** flagged a question-bank *library* — a different sense of the word (a shared bank of exam questions), unrelated to the book library; no overlap, nothing to reconcile.
