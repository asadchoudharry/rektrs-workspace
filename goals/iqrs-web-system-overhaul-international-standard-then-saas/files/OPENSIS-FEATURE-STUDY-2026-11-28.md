## 1. What openSIS is, in one paragraph

openSIS is the oldest-shaped of the three SISs in this batch: a US school-office system in the RosarioSIS mould — enrolment, scheduling, attendance, gradebook, report cards, transcripts, eligibility, parent/student portals — written in PHP over MySQL, in production use since the 2000s and now on Community Edition 9.3. Two things distinguish it from the earlier studies. First, it was designed from the start for a **district**: one installation holds many schools, with shared staff, shared students and a central administrator who moves between schools. Second, its centre of gravity has visibly shifted to the vendor's commercial side — the Community Edition today is a maintained free tier beside a SaaS product and paid editions that carry the roadmap (billing, discipline, state reporting, API, mobile app). Gibbon is a classroom system and RosarioSIS is an office system; openSIS is an office system whose free edition has been frozen in shape while its vendor sells the rest.

## 2. Project health — the honest verdict

**Verdict: alive, but thin, slow and security-reactive. Maintained, not developed. Do not plan around its roadmap.**

The evidence, as read on 2026-11-28:

- **Releases.** The README declares **Community Edition 9.3, release date 06/02/2026** (2 June 2026). GitHub's releases list, however, still shows **V9.2 (30 October 2025) as "Latest"** — the 9.3 line exists as the declared current version rather than a tidy GitHub release. The recent release notes (9.1, 9.2) say nothing beyond bug fixes and security fixes; 9.0 (PHP 8 / MySQL 8 compatibility) was the last release with platform content. Cadence is roughly annual, and feature-free.
- **Commits.** 498 commits on master in total (353 stars, 291 forks). 2026 activity comes in bursts, and the bursts are almost entirely security work: a run of automated code-quality/security fixes in March 2026, a security-fix merge in June, SQL-injection fixes on 29 September–5 October (including the fix tracked as CVE-2026-91107), and the most recent commit on **26 October 2026** — a README update adding a PHP-runtime security notice. Between bursts, the repository is quiet for weeks at a time.
- **Issues.** 17 issues stood open at the time of checking, and their ages are the clearest pace signal: a cluster of security reports filed **1 September 2026** (five SQL-injection reports, #473–#477) was still open nearly three months later; a stored remote-code-execution report (#478, 7 September) was still open; a report that upgrading 9.1 → 9.2/9.3 **"failed with blank page and destroys database"** (#479, 11 September) was still open; and an "incomplete fix" report on a previously patched CVE (#483, 22 October) was still open. Some reported problems *do* get fixed in code (the October SQL-injection commits), so the pattern is not abandonment — it is a small team fixing in batches, with issue replies lagging far behind the reports.
- **Security record.** This is the weakest part of the picture, and it matters for §3. Multiple CVE records published 30 September 2026 (a family of SQL-injection disclosures across student search, custom fields, student save and gradebook assignments) state that the project was informed through issue reports **but had not responded** at publication time; an authenticated path-traversal in the messaging attachment download (CVE-2026-11944, public exploit July 2026) and repeated cross-record authorisation defects (an incorrect-access-control flaw rated 8.1 in versions ≤ 9.2; IDOR reports in messaging and staff/school records in 2026) complete a pattern: the classic codebase's application-level checks fail regularly, and fixes arrive slowly.

**What this means for us:** openSIS is a legitimate *feature reference* — its workflows are battle-tested by years of school use — but it is not a healthy upstream. Nothing in this study should be read as "adopt the platform"; everything adoptable is an idea to implement independently, which is our standing rule anyway.

## 3. ⭐ The headline — multi-institution in one install, and the multi-tenancy lesson

RosarioSIS already showed us multi-school-in-one-instance (its study §3.1, point 8). openSIS is the worked example of how such a model actually behaves day to day, because the district is its home terrain. Its mechanics, in our own words:

- **One install, one database, a school column.** A `schools` table holds each institution; nearly every operational table (periods, calendars, courses, course periods, enrolments, attendance codes…) carries a `SCHOOL_ID` (and school-year) column. There is no per-school database or schema.
- **A "current school" workspace.** Every user works inside one selected school at a time. An administrator — who can access *any* school in the system — picks the school from a pull-down at the top and the whole workspace refreshes into that school's context; school year and marking period switch the same way. Central administration is therefore not a separate console; it is the same screens with a wider school list.
- **Cross-school staff are first-class.** A staff member is one record; a relationship table ties staff to schools per school year, with start/end dates and a per-school role. A teacher who works at two schools has one login and two assignments, and login logic itself consults those relationship rows to decide which schools are live for that person.
- **Cross-school students are enrolments, not duplicates.** A student is one record; enrolment is a row per school and year. Transfers are new enrolment rows, and the vendor's current documentation describes the same idea as a "home school" with additional "away schools" for a student enrolled in more than one. Parents link to children across schools through the people-join, so one parent login sees children at different schools of the same install.
- **District templating and shared configuration.** A **Copy School** function clones a school's setup — school periods, marking periods, report-card grade and comment codes, eligibility activity codes, attendance codes, grade levels — as the starting point for a new school. Calendar events can be marked **system-wide** so they appear in every school. Custom student fields can be scoped **"All Schools"** (district-standard) or left school-local — a small, explicit answer to "which configuration is central and which is local."

### 3.1 The two philosophies, compared honestly

| Question | openSIS model (school column + app filtering) | Rektrs ERP model (PostgreSQL row-level security) |
|---|---|---|
| What is the boundary? | A `SCHOOL_ID` value that every query is *expected* to filter by | A tenant context the **database itself** enforces on every read/write, whether or not the application remembers |
| Who is inside the boundary? | One district/management that owns all the schools and trusts its central admins | Mutually unrelated customer schools that must never see each other |
| Cross-school people | Native and cheap — shared staff, shared students, one parent view across schools | Deliberately hard across tenants; easy only *within* a tenant |
| Cost of one forgotten filter | A cross-school data leak (see §2: this failure mode recurs in openSIS's CVE history) | Nothing — the policy still refuses the rows |
| Per-customer lifecycle | Not a concept: no provisioning, entitlements, per-school export/offboarding | Built in: provisioning, entitlements, expiring exports, destruction receipts (gap audit §2, rows 19/32) |
| Operational complexity | Very low — one database, one backup, trivial reporting across schools | Higher — tenant context must be carried everywhere, and cross-tenant reporting is a platform feature, not a query |

**Where openSIS's simpler model is enough.** When all schools in the install belong to **one owner** — a trust, a diocese, a small chain — whose administrators are trusted and whose schools actively share staff and students. In that world, cross-school visibility is a *feature*, the blast radius of a filtering bug is internal, and RLS-grade isolation buys little. This is a real market in Pakistan too: school groups running 2–5 campuses under one management.

**Where RLS wins.** The moment the system is sold to **unrelated schools** — our SaaS. Then the boundary is a legal and commercial promise, not a convenience: a single missed filter in a shared-schema system is a breach between customers (openSIS's own security record is the standing demonstration of how often that happens in a long-lived PHP codebase), auditors and enterprise customers ask how isolation is *enforced* rather than *intended*, and per-tenant export/offboarding needs a hard edge to cut along. It is telling that even OS4ED's own pricing concedes the gradient: its top Enterprise tier sells **"data isolation with dedicated database"** as the premium upgrade — the vendor itself treats shared-schema as the cheap tier, not the safe tier.

**The lesson for the Rektrs ERP (the actionable headline):** adopt openSIS's district **workspace patterns**, not its isolation philosophy — and notice that the two compose cleanly. In our architecture the natural mapping is: **tenant = a school group** (the RLS boundary, one customer contract), **school = a scoping column inside the tenant** (openSIS's model, fenced by RLS at the group boundary). That gives a school group the switcher, the copy-school templating, system-wide events, All-Schools field scoping and shared staff/parents across its campuses — while two unrelated groups remain database-separated. openSIS proves the workspace patterns are what district users actually live in; our RLS keeps the promise openSIS cannot make. IQRS itself is a single school today, so for IQRS this is not a build item — it is a tenancy-design note for the ERP, recorded here while the evidence is fresh.

## 4. The other named specials

### 4.1 Eligibility — same destination, a different engine from RosarioSIS

RosarioSIS (study #2) *derives* eligibility: its Activities module reads the gradebook's pass state and computes who may participate — no human entry, always current, but only as good as the gradebook's currency and blind to anything that is not a grade.

openSIS runs eligibility as a **weekly attestation workflow**:

- The school defines its **activities** and a set of **eligibility codes**, and the administrator sets **Entry Times** — the weekly window in which eligibility must be entered.
- Each week, every teacher opens the eligibility entry screen for their class and assigns each student a code for that week. The entry can be manual, or the teacher can press **"Use Gradebook Grades"** and have the gradebook compute the codes — teacher judgement and automation are both first-class, per week, per class.
- The office side closes the loop: activities are enrolled in bulk (add an activity to a whole group in one action), a per-student screen shows activities and the current timeframe's eligibility, reports list every course and eligibility grade per student for a timeframe — and a **Teacher Completion Report** names the teachers who have *not* entered eligibility for the selected range.

**Judgement.** Neither engine is strictly better; they encode different trust. Rosario's computed rule costs zero labour and cannot be gamed, but it cannot see behaviour, effort or attendance. openSIS's attestation can encode exactly those — which is how eligibility is actually decided for school sports in Pakistan — at the price of weekly teacher labour and an office chase, which is precisely what its completion report exists to run. This **refines** the Rosario study's row 3 rather than replacing it (it is not double-counted in §6's new-flag tally): whichever engine we build, the two borrowable details are the *optional* gradebook-computed default under a teacher's confirmation, and the completion report — "who hasn't certified yet" is the question the office actually asks.

### 4.2 Transcripts — cumulative production, and the transfer-student piece

openSIS's transcript is a batch production job over the student's accumulated record: transcripts print for **any number of students at once**, the operator selects **which marking periods** to include, and student search for the batch can be filtered by course, GPA range, class rank or failure state. A dedicated calculation step computes weighted/unweighted GPA and class rank across the record (course weights act as multipliers; tied GPAs share a rank), and parents/students can print their own transcript from the portal for chosen periods.

The quietly important piece is **historical marking periods**. When a student transfers in mid-education, staff create historical periods for the years spent elsewhere and type in the prior school's results — course name, percentage, letter grade, grade-point values, grade scale — so the student's transcript is *complete across schools*, not just since admission. Neither earlier study surfaced an equivalent, and neither of our systems models it: both treat the academic record as beginning at admission.

**PK judgement.** The GPA/class-rank machinery itself stays **not relevant** — BISE mark sheets and positions govern reporting here (the same ruling as the Rosario study's row 13). Batch production with selectable periods matches machinery IQRS already runs for report cards. The adoptable slice is the historical backfill (§6, row 5): student mobility between Pakistani schools is constant, and a cumulative record that starts at admission is a real, recurring office workaround.

### 4.3 Scheduling — FET, RosarioSIS and openSIS on one line

The three scheduling ideas in this batch solve different problems, and openSIS completes the picture:

- **FET** (referenced by a RosarioSIS add-on): **generates the master timetable itself** — which course periods exist, with which teacher in which room — from constraints. Neither SIS does this.
- **RosarioSIS**: a **placement engine** over existing course periods — requests in, placements out, a test-mode re-run on every request save, unfulfilled-request and incomplete-schedule hunting; positioned as fast enough to re-run through the day.
- **openSIS**: the **same placement family**, independently built — student requests (entered individually, or in bulk through **Group Requests**), a **Run Scheduler** that places every student from their requests and reports conflicts and zero-seat courses (its own handbook warns a run can take several minutes — slower than Rosario's positioning), the **same test-mode check on request save**, and **Unfilled Requests** / **Incomplete Schedules** reports. On top of the engine it adds direct bulk operations the Rosario study did not emphasise: **Group Schedule** (place a selected group of students into a course in one action, bypassing the request cycle) and **Group Drops**, plus a **Requests Report** that totals demand per course expressly so the office can decide how many course periods a course needs — request data feeding master-timetable planning.

**One line:** *FET creates the timetable; RosarioSIS and openSIS fill it with students; openSIS additionally lets the office bulk-place and bulk-drop groups by hand, and reads request demand back into timetable planning.*

For our systems this changes nothing about the ranking — the placement engine remains the Rosario study's row-1 flag and is **not re-flagged** — but openSIS usefully specifies the office-grade version of it: in a Pakistani school, most placement is not individual student choice at all, it is the office putting a whole class into a subject group. Group Schedule/Group Drops and the demand report are the parts of the pattern shaped for that reality, and they belong in the requirements when the scheduler is built.

## 5. Anything else openSIS has that both prior studies lacked

- **Student Goals & Progresses (CE).** The student record carries **goals with dated progress entries**, and a dedicated print produces students' goals and progresses over a chosen date range. Gibbon's nearest constructs are markbook personal targets and full IEPs; RosarioSIS has neither. This is the lightweight end of the individual-needs spectrum — a goal and a progress trail on the record, without an IEP apparatus. New, minor, and flagged in §6.
- **Advanced Report (CE).** A field-picker report for students, staff and parents: choose the records, tick the fields (demographics, address, medical, custom fields, schedule), print. It is real but shallow next to a query builder — IQRS already has a custom report builder (FC-13) and the ERP's lack of one was already flagged by the Gibbon study (row 19), so this is **not re-flagged**; noted only so the ERP's future builder can note the field-picker as the minimum viable shape.
- **Letters and labels (CE).** Generated letters to parents carrying per-student details, plus mailing/student labels. Office convenience; our communication and front-office surfaces cover the underlying need. Not flagged.
- **Field-level security — claimed, not verified.** Older vendor descriptions advertise field-level user security; the vendor's current help documentation describes profile/page-level view/edit permissions instead. On the evidence available, the field-level claim cannot be confirmed for CE 9.3, so it is recorded here as an open question, **not** flagged as an adoption.

And the absences, stated plainly because vendor marketing blurs them: **the Community Edition has no student billing, no discipline module, no library, no cafeteria, no transport, no hostel and no payroll.** Discipline, billing and state reporting exist in the vendor's paid editions/SaaS. A reader of opensis.com alone would overestimate CE by roughly the whole commercial catalogue.

## 7. ⭐ The adoption shortlist — LACK and worth it (genuinely new items only)

1. **A district workspace layer for the ERP: tenant = school group** (ERP) — school switcher for group admins, Copy School setup templating (periods, marking periods, grade/comment/attendance/eligibility codes, grade levels), system-wide events and notices, custom fields scoped "All Schools" vs school-local, staff assigned across the group's schools under one login, parents seeing children across the group's schools. All of it *inside* one RLS tenant, so adopting it costs nothing in isolation. This is openSIS's one structural idea that neither prior study could give us, and it converts our tenancy from "one school per tenant" into "one *customer* per tenant" — which is how school groups actually buy.
2. **Historical grade backfill for transfer students** (both, minor) — historical periods on the student record carrying prior-school results (course, percentage, letter grade, grade-point values, scale), so transcripts and cumulative reports are complete across a student's school changes. Small, bounded, and aimed at a weekly office workaround in any school with mid-year admissions.
3. **Student Goals & Progresses** (both, minor) — goals with dated progress entries on the student record, printable by range. The light end of the individual-needs spectrum: worth having in its own right, and a natural first step on the road to the Gibbon study's IEP flag rather than a substitute for it.

**Addendum (not counted above, because the Rosario study owns the flag):** when the eligibility flag (Rosario row 3) is built, build the openSIS variant — weekly attestation windows, teacher-entered codes with an optional gradebook-computed default, and the Teacher Completion Report — and when the scheduler (Rosario row 1) is built, include Group Schedule/Group Drops and the request-demand report in its requirements (§4.3).

**Deliberately not shortlisted:** US state reporting (row 11 — wrong regulator, and a paid tier besides), GPA/honour-roll machinery (row 8 — board reporting governs), and field-level security (§5 — an unverified vendor claim for CE, not a studied workflow).

## 8. Balance — where openSIS is weaker than both of ours

- **The Community Edition is far narrower than the brand.** No billing, no discipline, no library, no cafeteria, no transport, no hostel, no payroll in CE 9.3 — several of those are modules both of our systems run today (IQRS: fees, payroll, transport, hostel, cafeteria, library all built; ERP: finance and HR/payroll source-complete, transport partial).
- **Pakistan rails: absent, as with both earlier studies.** No JazzCash/Easypaisa, no local SMS-gateway story, no WhatsApp channel, no board-pattern exam machinery (date sheets, admit cards, BISE-style mark sheets), no PK fee practice (fines, discounts, instalments, arrears).
- **Native mobile apps: none in CE.** The vendor's mobile app belongs to the SaaS product. Ours are published on Google Play (IQRS Connect + Workspace) with offline sync and QR attendance.
- **Multi-tenancy as a promise: it cannot make one.** Its multi-school model is a district convenience with application-level filtering and a CVE history to match (§2, §3.1); the ERP's forced RLS, provisioning, entitlements and tenant data-lifecycle have no openSIS counterpart — and the vendor sells database isolation as its own top-tier upgrade.
- **Digital ID cards with QR and a governed issue/revoke/replace register**: built in the IQRS overhaul; no CE equivalent.
- **Project trajectory (§2):** slow, security-reactive maintenance with months-old open vulnerability reports is a risk profile neither of our products carries; as a reference it is valuable, as a dependency it would be a liability.

**Bottom line:** Gibbon taught us classroom ideas and RosarioSIS taught us office machinery; openSIS teaches **district shape** — how several schools live inside one system as a workspace — while simultaneously demonstrating, in its own security record, why the isolation underneath that workspace must be enforced by the database and not by convention. The adoption list is therefore short and structural: take the district workspace for the ERP's group tenants, take two small student-record completions (historical backfill, goals & progresses), and leave the rest. Implemented independently, under our own licences, as always.
