# Moodle Feature Study — the LMS feature universe, and what our two school systems can learn from it

**Date:** 2026-11-28 · **Type:** STUDY-ONLY feature reference · **Subject:** Moodle (github.com/moodle/moodle, moodle.org / docs.moodle.org), the world's most widely deployed open-source learning management system, in continuous production use across schools and universities for two decades.

> **⚠️ LICENCE NOTE — read first.** Moodle is licensed **GPL-3.0**. This study is a *feature and workflow reference only*: it describes what Moodle does, in our own words, so we can decide which **ideas** are worth building ourselves. **No Moodle code, text, or assets have been copied into any Rektrs/IQRS tree, and none may be** — GPL-3.0 code reuse would contaminate our proprietary products. Anything adopted from this study must be implemented independently, the same discipline applied to the Gibbon study and the legacy-system feature references.

**Our two systems, as mapped for comparison:**
- **Rektrs ERP** = `rektrs-school-erp-lms` per `REKTRS-SCHOOL-ERP-GAP-AUDIT-2026-11-23.md` (17 source-complete / 13 partial / 4 missing of 34 PreSkool areas; 0 acceptance-complete). Its LMS line (audit row 4) is **PARTIAL**: quiz attempts, assignment submission/grading, SCORM quarantine and xAPI/LTI ingestion exist in source; there is **no standalone question-bank library**, and quiz-engine scoring sits behind adapters.
- **IQRS** = the live Smart School system as reshaped by the PreSkool overhaul. Its LMS is the **Online Course add-on** (KEEP-OURS in the 2026-11-17 disposition plan), which is **installed but config-hidden** — present in code, dormant in production until licensed/configured. Its installed controllers (verified in the staging tree) are: course catalogue with categories and tags, sections, lessons, in-course quizzes, course exams with their own per-exam questions, assignments with evaluation, course certificates, course reports, and offline-payment course sales. Around it, the wider Smart School system now has a Question Bank + Online Examination (overhaul Cards 1–2), school-level rewards/badges (FC-1) and a custom report builder (FC-13) — none of them wired into the LMS itself.

## 1. What Moodle is, in one paragraph

Moodle is a course-centred LMS: everything hangs off a *course* — a structured space of sections filled with activities (things students do) and resources (things students read/watch) — with one gradebook per course collecting every graded activity, a shared question bank feeding its quiz engine, and site-wide machinery (enrolments, groups, cohorts, competencies, badges, reporting) wrapped around the courses. Where Gibbon's centre of gravity is the teacher's planner and markbook inside a school ERP, Moodle's is the **self-contained online course** — it is the reference implementation of "LMS" as a category, which is why this study maps the LMS layer only, not school administration (Moodle has no fees, payroll, transport or admissions machinery of its own; schools pair it with an SIS — Gibbon itself ships a Moodle integration for exactly this reason).

## 2. Moodle's LMS feature universe (our own words)

**Courses and content.** Courses sit in a category tree and are laid out in sections (by topic or by week). Teachers fill sections with resources — files, folders, pages, multi-page "books", links, labels — and with interactive content authored in a built-in **content bank**, notably **H5P** packages (interactive video, branching scenarios, interactive presentations and quizzes) whose learner interactions can emit xAPI statements. Courses can be copied, backed up, restored and shared as templates.

**Quiz engine.** The quiz is a timed, attempt-managed assessment player: multiple attempts with configurable grading basis (highest, average, first, last), per-question *behaviours* (immediate or deferred feedback, adaptive mode with penalties for retries, interactive multiple-tries, certainty-based marking where students state how sure they are), time limits, question-per-page layouts, review windows controlling what students see and when, per-user and per-group overrides (extra time, extra attempts, different dates), and access rules including password, network restriction and Safe Exam Browser integration.

**Question bank.** Questions live in a bank *separate from any quiz*, organised in a hierarchy of categories that can be scoped to one course, a course category, or the whole site — scoping decides who may reuse them. Questions are versioned (edits create new versions; quizzes can pin or follow versions), carry a draft/ready status, show usage statistics, and move between systems through import/export formats (including Moodle XML and GIFT). **Question types** are broad: multiple choice (single/multi), true/false, matching, short answer, numerical with units and tolerances, **calculated** questions whose numbers are generated from datasets per attempt, cloze/embedded-answer passages mixing several sub-questions in one text, drag-and-drop onto images and into text, select-the-missing-word, random short-answer matching, essay (manually graded), and description. **Randomisation** is structural: a quiz can draw a random subset from a category at attempt time, shuffle choices, and combine fixed and random slots.

**Gradebook.** Each course has a gradebook organised as a tree of categories, each with its own aggregation rule chosen from eight methods (mean, weighted mean, simple weighted mean, median, lowest, highest, mode, natural sum) or a custom formula; weights, extra credit and drop-the-lowest are supported. Grades can be numbers, custom **scales** (ordered word lists) or **letters**; **outcomes** (standards) can be attached to activities and reported alongside scores. There is a full audit history of grade changes, single-student and single-item views, bulk import/export, pass thresholds, and hidden/locked grades.

**Assignments.** Submission types include files, online text and media; teachers can require acceptance of a submission statement, set cut-off dates and extensions, allow resubmission (reopened attempts), collect **group submissions** with per-group grading, mark **blind** (student identity hidden), allocate markers and run a marking workflow (not marked → in marking → ready for release → released), and give feedback as comments, annotated PDFs, audio or files. Two **advanced grading methods** — **rubrics** (criterion grids with level descriptions) and **marking guides** (criteria with guidance and max scores) — are reusable as templates across courses and feed the gradebook directly.

**Workshop (peer assessment).** A five-phase activity — setup, submission, assessment, grading evaluation, closed — in which students submit work, are allocated peers' submissions (randomly or manually), and assess them against a rubric or other grading strategy. Students receive **two grades**: one for their submission and one for the *quality of their own assessing*, computed in the evaluation phase by comparing their judgements against the consensus, with teacher override throughout. Example submissions can be provided for calibration.

**Lesson and pathways.** A lesson activity builds branching scenarios: content pages and question pages linked by jumps, so a student's answers route them through remedial or advanced paths, with flow control and practice modes.

**Completion and conditional release.** Any activity can carry completion conditions (viewed, submitted, graded, a minimum grade, or manual self-marking), and course completion is defined as a set of such criteria. Completion state drives per-student and per-course **completion reports**, and a separate **restrict-access** layer can hide or release activities and sections based on date, grade, group, or completion of earlier items — the machinery behind drip-fed and mastery-based course designs.

**Competencies and learning plans.** Administrators define competency frameworks (hierarchical skill trees with proficiency scales), link competencies to courses and activities, and build a **learning plan** per student or cohort: a personal list of competencies to evidence, with teacher ratings, evidence attachments and a competency-breakdown report per course.

**Badges and certificates.** Badges are core: visual awards issued manually or automatically against criteria (activity/course completion, competency proficiency, profile fields), with expiry and Open Badges portability so learners can carry them to external backpacks. Printable **certificates** are not a core designer — they are delivered by widely used certificate plugins keyed off completion/gradebook state.

**Social and knowledge activities.** Forums come in several shapes (standard, Q&A where students must post before reading peers, one-discussion-per-person, blog-style), with subscriptions, ratings, pinning and whole-forum grading. Around them sit a long tail of activity types: **Choice** (a quick poll), **Feedback** (buildable surveys/course evaluations with templates and analysis), **Glossary** (student-contributed definitions with auto-linking into course text), **Database** (teacher-designed structured collections students populate), **Wiki**, **Chat**, and pre-built attitudinal surveys.

**SCORM, H5P and standards.** SCORM 1.2/2004 packages play with attempt tracking and grade pass-through; H5P content (above) reports through xAPI; the platform both consumes and provides **LTI** tools, ingests xAPI into a log store, and exposes a full web-services/REST API.

**Enrolment and audiences.** Courses admit learners through many enrolment methods: manual, self-enrolment (optionally keyed), **cohort sync** (membership of a site-wide cohort automatically enrols its members), guest access, paid enrolment, meta-links between courses, and external sources (LDAP, external database, flat file, IMS). Enrolments can expire with notifications. Inside a course, students can be split into **groups** (with groupings, separate/visible group modes, group overrides on assessments and group messaging), while **cohorts** organise audiences across the whole site.

**Reporting and analytics.** Beyond per-course logs, live logs and participation reports, Moodle has a configurable **report builder** (drag-together columns, filters, audiences and scheduled sending) and an **analytics engine** with predictive models — most notably identifying students at risk of dropping out from engagement patterns — surfaced as "insights" with suggested actions to teachers, plus event-monitoring rules that fire notifications when conditions occur.

**Mobile and notifications.** An official mobile app downloads courses for offline use (including offline quiz and assignment attempts that sync later), receives push notifications, and respects per-activity mobile availability. Messaging, deadline timelines on the dashboard, and configurable notification preferences (web, email, mobile) tie the course layer together.

**Roles, AI, and the virtual classroom.** A capability-based permission system scopes roles by context (site, category, course, even a single activity). A recent AI subsystem lets sites connect a provider and offer generate/explain/summarise actions inside courses. Moodle ships **BigBlueButton as its built-in virtual-classroom activity** (present but disabled until an administrator enables it) — one line only here; BigBlueButton receives its own study in this batch.

## 4. ⭐ The adoption shortlist — LACK and worth it

1. **Configurable gradebook aggregation engine** (both systems) — categories with weights and aggregation rules over *all* graded LMS work, not just exam totals. *What it takes:* data-model work (grade items, category tree, aggregation config per course) plus the grader surface. This is the LMS counterpart of the markbook the Gibbon study flagged — Moodle's version is deeper (see §5).
2. **A shared, versioned question bank** (both; IQRS partially there) — one bank scoped by subject/course/site with category trees, question versioning, sharing and import/export, feeding every quiz and online exam; calculated and cloze question types land with it. *What it takes:* data-model work (bank scoping + versioning) over question tables both systems already have.
3. **Completion tracking + conditional release** (both) — per-student completion state on activities/courses, then date/grade/completion-gated release of content. *What it takes:* data-model work (completion state + condition rules) plus surfaces; it is also the trigger layer that makes certificates, badges and parent progress views honest.
4. **Rubrics & marking guides as reusable grading methods** (both) — criterion-based marking for assignments and projects, defined once as templates, feeding the gradebook. *What it takes:* light data-model work (criterion definitions + filled instances) plus the grading surface. Already flagged via Gibbon; Moodle shows the gradebook-integrated form of the idea.
5. **Workshop-style peer assessment** (both) — phased submit → allocate → assess → evaluate, with a grade for the quality of a student's own reviewing. *What it takes:* the heaviest single build on this list — real data-model work (phases, allocations, dual grades) — but it is one self-contained activity type. Gibbon's crowd assessment is the shallow version of this; Moodle's is the deep one.
6. **Competency frameworks + learning plans** (ERP partially framed; IQRS entirely) — skill trees linked to activities, per-student plans with evidence and proficiency ratings. *What it takes:* data-model work (frameworks, plans, evidence links) plus reporting surfaces.
7. **H5P interactive content** (both) — interactive video/branching/presentation content authored in a content bank, reporting through the xAPI plumbing the ERP already ingests. *What it takes:* mostly integration and surface work (player + content storage); the content-bank model is small.
8. **Quiz item analysis** (both) — per-question facility/discrimination statistics over stored attempts, so teachers can see which questions misbehave. *What it takes:* **surface-only** — a read-side statistics layer over attempt data both systems already store. The cheapest item on this list.
9. **Badges completed on the ERP side + LMS-linked criteria** (ERP; IQRS linking only) — the ERP's missing rewards module, with badge criteria hooked to completion (item 3) and Open Badges portability. *What it takes:* light data-model work; the module is already scoped as missing in the ERP's own audit.
10. **Course forums for the IQRS LMS** (minor) — a per-course discussion/announcement space with the Q&A pattern (post before you can read peers). *What it takes:* surface work plus a small discussion model inside the dormant add-on, if and when the add-on is activated.
11. **Feedback/course-evaluation surveys** (minor, both) — buildable end-of-course surveys with reusable templates and simple analysis. *What it takes:* surface work over a small template/response model.

## 5. Moodle ↔ Gibbon — where they overlap, who is deeper

The Gibbon study (same date, this folder) flagged several teaching-and-learning ideas; this study deliberately does not re-flag them as new. Where the two overlap:

- **Continuous markbook (Gibbon) vs gradebook (Moodle): Moodle is deeper.** Gibbon's markbook is a longitudinal record with per-column visibility; Moodle adds a full aggregation engine — category trees, eight aggregation methods, formulas, extra credit, outcomes — underneath the same idea. Adopt the Gibbon-shaped *surface* (teachers and parents live in it) with Moodle-shaped *machinery* under it.
- **Rubrics: comparable, Moodle slightly deeper in integration.** Both accumulate rubric results over time; Moodle's rubrics are a gradebook grading method with reusable cross-course templates and outcome reporting.
- **Crowd assessment (Gibbon) vs Workshop (Moodle): Moodle is far deeper.** Gibbon shares work to an audience for discussion; Moodle runs a phased assessment workflow and grades the grading itself.
- **Learning outcomes (Gibbon) vs competencies + learning plans (Moodle): Moodle is deeper.** Gibbon attaches outcomes to units and rolls up coverage; Moodle adds per-student learning plans, evidence and proficiency tracking.
- **Badges: Moodle is deeper.** Both define and award badges; Moodle adds automated criteria (completion, competency) and Open Badges portability.
- **Free Learning (Gibbon) vs Moodle itself: not comparable** — Gibbon's is a light self-directed add-on; Moodle is the full LMS, and Gibbon ships an integration that hands learners to Moodle, which settles which system the category belongs to.

Everything else in §4 (question bank, completion/conditional release, H5P, item analysis, forums, feedback) has **no Gibbon counterpart** — it is net-new territory from this study, not double-counted.

## 6. Balance — where our systems already stand fine against Moodle

- **Moodle is not a school system.** It has no admissions, fees, payroll, attendance registers, transport, hostel or parent-billing — the operational depth both of ours are built on (ERP finance/attendance source-complete; IQRS's full Smart School operations). Moodle's LMS census should complete our platforms, not replace them.
- **Certificates:** IQRS already issues course certificates from its (dormant) add-on and runs a certificates module; the ERP gates LMS certificates on its published gradebook — Moodle core itself leaves certificates to plugins, so neither of ours is behind the category here.
- **Live classes:** Moodle's built-in classroom is BigBlueButton (separate study); IQRS already carries Gmeet/Zoom add-ons, and the ERP has their mappings — different engines, same job.
- **Pakistan rails:** Moodle has no JazzCash/Easypaisa, no Sendqon-style SMS delivery, no board-pattern exam machinery — our systems' home ground, untouched by anything in this census.
- **Mobile:** Moodle's app is a course player; ours are full school apps (IQRS's are published with offline sync and QR attendance), so the LMS-in-app gap is about surfacing the course layer, not building an app.

**Bottom line:** Moodle's lead over us is the *connected learning loop* — a shared question bank feeding rich quizzes, completion state driving release and recognition, one configurable gradebook aggregating everything, and competencies tracked per student. Our lead is everything around the course: the school itself. The shortlist in §4 borrows the loop, in pieces sized to our two platforms, implemented independently under our own licences.
